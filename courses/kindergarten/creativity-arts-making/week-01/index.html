Day 1: Colors Have Stories

Explore primary and secondary colors with paper cards.

Red, yellow and blue are often called primary colors in elementary paint mixing. Red and yellow can make orange; blue and yellow can make green; blue and red can make purple. Paint colors vary with material, so pictures are a safe first experiment.

Example: Red and yellow pretend paint can make orange, whereas blue and yellow often make green. Real pigments may differ, so art is a place to observe rather than guess one perfect shade.

Try it together: Choose two color cards, predict their pretend mix, then compare with the Color-Mixing Studio. Describe one difference between your prediction and the result.

My independent picture challenge: Draw three pairs of paint circles and label possible mixes using words, signs or colored pencils.

Caregiver teaching note: No real paint is required. If used, verify age-safe materials and allergies; never taste supplies.

My evidence: Draw three color pairs and their possible mixes.

Day 2: Shapes Everywhere

Identify and arrange circles, triangles, squares and rectangles.

We see shapes in signs, windows and artwork. Cut-out shapes should be prepared by adults if scissors are needed. Choose paper shapes to build a friendly imaginary town.

Example: A square window, a triangle roof and a circular sun can form a picture from three different shapes. Shapes can overlap to create more interesting scenes.

Try it together: Arrange three drawn shapes into a pretend house; ask which shape should appear behind another.

My independent picture challenge: Create a new picture using four shapes, and explain one choice.

Caregiver teaching note: Let children point or arrange precut adult-prepared paper; avoid sharp scissors and choking-size pieces.

My evidence: Draw a shape house and count its windows.

Day 3: Patterns That Repeat

Build a two-color or two-shape repeating pattern.

A repeating pattern follows a rule such as red-blue-red-blue. Look for what comes next and explain the pattern using words, pointing or pictures.

Example: An AB pattern can repeat circle-square-circle-square; an AAB rule can repeat circle-circle-square. Both have a predictable unit.

Try it together: Build six pictured symbols in an AB arrangement, then change the rule and describe what comes next.

My independent picture challenge: Draw eight shapes in a repeating pattern and outline its repeat unit.

Caregiver teaching note: Guide learners toward noticing a rule, not simply choosing a color that looks attractive.

My evidence: Draw an ABAB pattern with six symbols.

Day 4: Art Makes Feelings Visible

Express feelings through safe color and lines.

Artists may use colors, lines and shapes to share ideas or feelings. A color does not mean the same emotion for every person; ask what someone's art means to them.

Example: Long, short, straight and wavy lines can suggest very different ideas. A learner may associate yellow with sadness or happiness; feelings in art are personal.

Try it together: Draw one straight line and one looping line. Ask what each suggests, without declaring an emotion correct or incorrect.

My independent picture challenge: Make two abstract pictures with different line styles and title them.

Caregiver teaching note: Allow children to communicate feelings privately or imaginatively; never require disclosure of personal distress.

My evidence: Draw two different line styles to express an idea.

Day 5: My First Gallery

Choose and describe an artwork.

Gather several drawings and choose one to explain. Describe the shapes, colors and pattern you used, and listen respectfully to other artists.

Example: A gallery label helps visitors know what a drawing represents. Artists may use a name, initial or chosen pseudonym rather than personal details.

Try it together: Compare a label with only a tiny scribble to one with a large artwork title and easy-to-read icon.

My independent picture challenge: Make a picture label with a title, two techniques and a kind explanation.

Caregiver teaching note: Do not upload identifiable child art or names without family permission; invite respectful listening.

My evidence: Create a picture label with title and artist.
