Day 1: Mix and Compare Colors

Predict color combinations using picture palettes.

Pretend palettes help us compare expected colors without paint. If using real paint, caregivers check that materials are age-safe, non-toxic and suitable for the learner.

Example: Pretend pigment colors can be compared in a three-column prediction chart: two starting colors and the mixed color. With real materials, brightness and shade may vary.

Try it together: Predict red plus blue, then test the visual studio and explain why a physical paint mix might not exactly match a screen.

My independent picture challenge: Draw three mix trials with starting colors, prediction and imagined outcome.

Caregiver teaching note: Explain that the game models paint combinations, not additive colored light; art supplies must be checked by adults.

My evidence: Create a paper color-mix prediction chart.

Day 2: Build a Pattern Quilt

Make more complex repeating arrangements.

An AAB pattern might repeat yellow-yellow-blue. Try predicting two more pieces and designing a quilt with drawings or reusable cards.

Example: An AAB quilt tile might show star-star-heart, repeated three times. The same rule can use shapes, pictures or sounds.

Try it together: Build an AAB row and ask which symbol occupies positions four, five and six.

My independent picture challenge: Design a nine-tile paper quilt using an AAB or ABB rule; mark one repeating group.

Caregiver teaching note: Use large drawn tiles or spoken patterns for accessible participation; no fabric or needles are needed.

My evidence: Draw an AAB pattern of nine shapes.

Day 3: Texture Detectives

Describe surfaces through observation.

Textures can look smooth, bumpy, rough or soft. Use safe paper rubbings or pictures; avoid unknown plants and sharp surfaces.

Example: Texture can be described visually as bumpy, smooth or ridged. A crayon rubbing may reveal texture, but pictures alone can also tell a story.

Try it together: Compare three imagined texture cards and sort them by how their surfaces might feel.

My independent picture challenge: Create a four-square texture sketch using lines, dots, waves and crosshatching.

Caregiver teaching note: Do not encourage touching unknown surfaces, plants or materials; offer vision-friendly verbal descriptions.

My evidence: Draw two textures and give each a word.

Day 4: Strong Paper Structures

Plan and improve a paper tower.

Folded paper can become a model wall or tower. A stable base can help the structure stand. Test only paper models and avoid hot glue, sharp craft knives and small choking hazards.

Example: A wide folded-paper base can sometimes support a taller paper structure than a narrow one. Testing models gives us evidence for revision.

Try it together: Draw two pretend towers with different bases and predict which will stand more easily.

My independent picture challenge: Design a folded-paper tower on paper, then change one feature to make its base more stable.

Caregiver teaching note: Use caregiver-approved paper, with adult handling of cutting tools; no hot glue or climbing.

My evidence: Draw a paper tower before and after improvement.

Day 5: Story Picture Panels

Tell a short beginning-middle-end story.

Choose three pictures to show a character trying something, meeting a challenge and finding a kind solution. Learners may tell the story aloud, in signs or by drawing.

Example: A three-panel story shows beginning, middle and end. A kind character can meet a small problem and solve it through cooperation.

Try it together: Order three mixed-up picture cards and explain what happens first, next and last.

My independent picture challenge: Illustrate a three-part story featuring a character who makes a thoughtful choice.

Caregiver teaching note: Allow speech, signs, narration or drawing. Encourage original characters rather than copied media artwork.

My evidence: Make three numbered panels and a story title.
