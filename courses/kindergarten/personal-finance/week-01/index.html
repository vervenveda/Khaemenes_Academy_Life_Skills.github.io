My Coins, Choices and Counting

Day 1: What Is Money?

Recognize money as one way to exchange for goods and services.

People use different currencies. Make paper pretend coins and count them; do not share real payment details.

Example: Money is one way people exchange for goods or services, but currencies differ across places. For our classroom, five drawn circles can represent five pretend tokens without using real coins.

Try it together: Count three paper tokens and exchange them for a picture of a pretend item. Ask what the tokens represent.

My independent picture challenge: Draw four different pretend tokens and label a shop picture using only fictional prices.

Caregiver teaching note: Never require real money, online accounts or payment details. Families have different financial resources; respect everyone's circumstances.

My evidence: Draw three pretend coins.

Day 2: Needs and Wants

Sort things we need and things we may want.

Water, food and shelter are needs. Toys may be wants. Families have different circumstances; never judge a person's resources.

Example: Clean water, food and a safe place to live are common needs. An extra decorative toy may be a want. The same object can meet different needs in different situations.

Try it together: Sort six picture cards into need, want or discuss. Ask why a warm coat might be important in cold weather.

My independent picture challenge: Draw one need and one want, explaining your choice through speech, pointing or writing.

Caregiver teaching note: Avoid judging families' purchases or circumstances. Allow nuance for disability supports and different communities.

My evidence: Draw one need and one want.

Day 3: Count a Pretend Shop

Use numbers one through five to count picture tokens.

Place paper number cards beside pretend items; a price is an example, not a real cost.

Example: A pretend apple costs two paper tokens and a pretend pear costs three. One paper token is a learning counter, not a real price.

Try it together: Match the numbers 1–5 to groups of drawn tokens, then count out the price of two imaginary items.

My independent picture challenge: Create three pretend price tags and draw the matching number of tokens below each.

Caregiver teaching note: Use large paper counters rather than real coins if choking or swallowing is a concern; no purchase is involved.

My evidence: Draw four paper tokens beside a pretend apple.

Day 4: Choose Within a Budget

Make a choice with limited pretend tokens.

If you have three picture tokens, choose an item that costs two, leaving one. A budget helps us plan and compare.

Example: With three pretend tokens, choosing an item costing two leaves one. Choosing a different item costing three leaves none. Both can be planned choices.

Try it together: Place three paper tokens on a table drawing. Compare two price cards and count how many remain after either choice.

My independent picture challenge: Draw a three-token budget and show two alternative purchases using pictures and subtraction.

Caregiver teaching note: Discuss tradeoffs without suggesting that having less money reflects poor choices or personal worth.

My evidence: Draw a three-token plan.

Day 5: My Thoughtful Shop

Make a respectful pretend-shop poster.

Offer useful pictures rather than real sales. No purchases, accounts or personal information are required.

Example: A pretend shop can display a paper apple, book and toy with picture prices. A welcoming sign can show everyone how to participate.

Try it together: Design three item cards, then take turns being a fictional customer and shopkeeper using paper tokens only.

My independent picture challenge: Make a pretend shop poster with three clear drawings and an inviting title.

Caregiver teaching note: Avoid real purchasing, advertisements, collection of private details or pressure to buy.

My evidence: Create a paper shop sign with three items.
