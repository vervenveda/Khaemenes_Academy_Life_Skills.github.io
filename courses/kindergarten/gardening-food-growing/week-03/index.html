Day 1: Choose My Garden Project

Select a safe picture garden to plan.

A final project can be a paper container garden, pretend vegetable bed or flower space. A caregiver checks any real-world gardening. Everyone can use pictures, words or gestures.

Example: A project can be a paper seed garden, a fictional flower bed or an accessible window-pot plan. Each option can demonstrate learning.

Try it together: Compare two imaginary plans and choose one goal, two steps and a safe role for an adult.

My independent picture challenge: Draw a project title, materials from paper and a finish picture.

Caregiver teaching note: Approve any practical activity first; do not require soil, real seeds or a home garden.

My evidence: Draw your chosen garden and its purpose.

Day 2: Count and Group Plants

Use numbers and categories in a garden plan.

Choose one to five pretend plant symbols. Group by type or color. A clear label helps another person understand your garden.

Example: A plot picture can show two red flowers and three yellow flowers, making five total plants.

Try it together: Count five symbols, group them by color and check that the groups add to the total.

My independent picture challenge: Create labels for two groups and write, point to or say the total.

Caregiver teaching note: Allow large cards, audio counting and flexible motor responses; paper icons are sufficient.

My evidence: Draw two groups and label their counts.

Day 3: Make a Garden Care Calendar

Plan age-safe observation reminders.

A calendar shows when to look at a plant and ask an adult about its needs. No child should handle chemicals or unknown supplies.

Example: A care calendar might say 'look at the leaf drawing on Monday' and 'ask an adult about watering on Thursday.' The calendar is a reminder, not a fixed plant prescription.

Try it together: Order three pretend care reminders and discuss why different plants may need different checks.

My independent picture challenge: Draw a five-day care chart with an observe symbol and adult-help symbol.

Caregiver teaching note: Check real plant requirements yourself; do not let a picture calendar authorize watering, fertilizers or chemicals.

My evidence: Create a three-day picture reminder plan.

Day 4: Improve With Feedback

Revise a plan for safety and inclusion.

Ask a trusted adult if your map is understandable, if paths are safe and whether all learners can participate. Change one part based on kind feedback.

Example: An adult might say a picture path is hard to follow. Making its line thicker can help more people understand the map.

Try it together: Ask a trusted reviewer what is clear and what might improve. Consider accessibility and labeling.

My independent picture challenge: Draw a before-and-after version of one garden-map feature.

Caregiver teaching note: Keep feedback specific and kind; learners decide how to represent ideas and can respond through gestures.

My evidence: Show one before-and-after change.

Day 5: Garden Learning Celebration

Present evidence and describe discoveries.

Share your map, count cards, journal pictures and a safe care plan. Explain what plants may need and how to observe safely. The quiz checks knowledge, not independent gardening competence.

Example: An exhibit might include a journal card, counting picture and a final garden map. Together, they show planning and observation skills.

Try it together: Practice describing one plant need, a safe choice and one thing learned across the three weeks.

My independent picture challenge: Make a portfolio cover with course title, learner, date, favorite drawing and caregiver review.

Caregiver teaching note: Do not share private addresses or learner photographs; celebrate effort rather than a perfect garden.

My evidence: Make a final picture portfolio with five examples.
