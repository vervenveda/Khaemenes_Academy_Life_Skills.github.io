Day 1: The Five-Day Garden Journal

Practice observing changes without inventing results.

An observation says what we actually noticed. Use pictures of an imaginary plant over five days and note changes carefully. If nothing changes, record that honestly.

Example: In a drawing, a seed may show no visible change on one day and a shoot on another. A good scientist records what they see, not what they hope to see.

Try it together: Arrange five fictional day cards, including a day with no change, and say which differences can be observed.

My independent picture challenge: Draw a five-box journal with dates or day numbers and observations.

Caregiver teaching note: Help distinguish made-up model data from actual observations; do not promise plants grow in five days.

My evidence: Make a five-day picture observation strip.

Day 2: Light and Shade

Compare two pretend growing spots.

Look at drawings of a bright spot and a shaded spot. Different plants prefer different conditions. Never look directly at the Sun.

Example: A sun-loving plant in a picture may thrive near light, while a shade-loving plant may be better in a sheltered area.

Try it together: Place two picture plants in bright and shaded zones, explaining why the adult should check care instructions.

My independent picture challenge: Create a light-and-shade map and use arrows to mark two possible planting places.

Caregiver teaching note: Never stare at the Sun; adults choose real growing locations and consider accessible observation.

My evidence: Draw two locations and compare them.

Day 3: Watering Choices

Choose safe watering and conservation plans.

A caregiver determines safe watering; too much or too little can harm a plant. Pretend water-drop cards can show amounts without using actual soil.

Example: Pretend water cards can show one drop, two drops and three drops. More is not always better for a plant.

Try it together: Compare picture pots showing very dry, suitable and flooded soil, and ask what information is missing.

My independent picture challenge: Draw a three-card sequence: observe, ask an adult, water only as advised.

Caregiver teaching note: Avoid real watering tasks without approval; some plants need very little water, and wet floors create slip risks.

My evidence: Compare one and three paper water drops.

Day 4: Garden Visitors

Recognize that insects and animals share habitats.

Some insects visit flowers and help move pollen. Watch unfamiliar insects from a distance. Do not capture or touch unknown creatures.

Example: A butterfly can visit flowers and may carry pollen. Not every insect visiting a garden is safe to approach or helpful to plants.

Try it together: Sort picture visitors into observe-from-a-distance and ask-an-adult questions.

My independent picture challenge: Illustrate a flower and one visitor, adding a symbol for respectful distance.

Caregiver teaching note: Do not handle bees, insects or unknown animals; support learners who are afraid of insects.

My evidence: Draw two garden visitors and their safe observation distance.

Day 5: Picture Garden Map

Map a garden with paths and labels.

Design a garden with a plant space, watering reminder, sunny or shady area and safe path. Include ways learners can participate without digging.

Example: A map can show a plant area, a path and a water symbol without representing a real home's private location.

Try it together: Build a paper map with large picture tiles and check whether a person can reach the pretend garden area.

My independent picture challenge: Draw a map with a labeled plant zone, path and shade or light symbol.

Caregiver teaching note: No real addresses or outdoor photos are needed; include wheelchair-friendly or seated participation.

My evidence: Create a four-label garden map.
