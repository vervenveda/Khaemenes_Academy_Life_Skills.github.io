Day 1: Ingredient Detectives

Compare choices before making a pretend recipe.

Families eat different foods. A responsible adult checks allergy information, food safety and what ingredients are available. Compare two picture ingredients for color, size and how they might be used. A child can draw instead of touching real food.

Example: Ingredients can be described by color, size and where they come from; photographs and drawings allow observation without eating.

Try it together: Compare three illustrated ingredients and name two observable properties without making health claims.

My independent picture challenge: Draw an ingredient detective card with a picture and two descriptive words.

Caregiver teaching note: Do not handle unknown foods or assume ingredients are allergy safe.

My evidence: Draw two ingredients and describe two differences.

Day 2: Tools and Safe Helpers

Match tools to tasks and assign grown-up roles.

A spoon may stir, a bowl may hold food, and a measuring cup helps compare amounts. A knife, heat source, blender and electrical appliance require responsible adult handling. A child can point, sort cards and ask questions.

Example: A wooden spoon, knife and oven have different uses and safety levels; kindergarten helpers should know which belong to adults.

Try it together: Sort paper tool cards into picture-only, ask-a-caregiver and adult-only categories.

My independent picture challenge: Draw one safe helper job and one adult tool.

Caregiver teaching note: Supervise any real work; do not permit knife, oven or appliance use.

My evidence: Match three pretend tools to tasks and circle adult-only equipment.

Day 3: Half and Whole

Explore halves and whole amounts with paper shapes.

Fold a paper circle into two equal parts. Each is one half of the whole. Compare two halves with one whole, and explain why equal parts matter. This is a drawing game, not a requirement to cut food or use real tools.

Example: A whole paper circle may be folded into two equal halves. Two equal halves together make a whole.

Try it together: Fold an adult-approved paper circle or use a pre-drawn diagram; compare equal and unequal parts.

My independent picture challenge: Draw one whole and two equal halves, labeling each.

Caregiver teaching note: No cutting or food splitting is necessary; offer tactile cards.

My evidence: Draw one whole paper circle and two equal halves.

Day 4: Sequence Challenge

Put four recipe picture cards in a safe order.

Plans have a beginning, middle and end. Try these picture steps: ask an adult to check ingredients, wash hands, let the adult handle any required cutting, and describe or serve the result safely. Different recipes may use different steps.

Example: An illustrated recipe might show wash hands, arrange cards and clean up in a fixed sequence. Order helps us remember safety.

Try it together: Scramble four picture steps and reorder them while explaining which must come first.

My independent picture challenge: Make a numbered three-step pretend recipe with a safe adult check.

Caregiver teaching note: Ensure no instruction invites unsupervised food preparation.

My evidence: Number four safety cards and explain which step comes first.

Day 5: My Family Food Story

Share a cultural food story respectfully.

Food traditions differ across families and communities. Ask a caregiver about a familiar dish if they wish to share. Explain three ingredients or features using pictures. Never assume everyone eats the same foods or that unfamiliar food is safe to taste.

Example: A family food story can describe a meaningful meal, celebration or memory without identifying private relatives or cultural traditions as better.

Try it together: Read a fictional meal story and point out the beginning, middle and end.

My independent picture challenge: Create three panels for an original or imagined food story.

Caregiver teaching note: Allow invented stories, respect cultural differences and family privacy.

My evidence: Make a three-picture food story, including one adult-approved safety note.
