Caregiver teaching guide · Five meaningful steps

Use this guide alongside each weekly lesson. First name the food or tool and ask what the child notices. Next show one safe action while describing it aloud. Then invite the child to point, sort, count, draw or explain rather than perform risky cooking tasks. Ask one open question, and finish by recording the child's own words or picture in the lesson portfolio.

1 · Observe

Show a clean bowl, measuring cup or food picture. Ask: “What do you see?” Offer two choices if language is still emerging.

2 · Model

Demonstrate a safe, unplugged, cool activity. Narrate: “The cup is half full; the other cup is empty.”

3 · Practice

Let the learner sort food pictures by color, count spoonfuls of pretend ingredients or match a tool to its purpose.

4 · Explain

Ask why hands are washed, why adults handle hot tools and how to choose a food that meets the family's dietary rules.

5 · Record

Save a drawing or caregiver observation. Celebrate effort without confusing stickers with verified mastery.

Dietary adaptations: use pictures and hypothetical ingredient lists for restricted foods. Never ask a child to taste a known allergen, handle raw ingredients unsupervised or demonstrate unsafe substitutions. The dietary selector supplies planning cautions, not allergen certification.

Why cooking matters

Cooking is a practical skill that combines safety, planning, measurement, nutrition and cooperation. Before preparing food, understand what you will make, read ingredient labels, consider dietary needs, and identify possible hazards. Good cooks prepare their materials before starting, clean their hands and work areas, and make choices that protect everyone sharing a meal. Learning slowly and reflecting on each task are more important than speed.

A useful recipe tells you what to gather and the sequence of preparation. Meal planning adds questions about household preferences, budget, available equipment and timing. Practice can begin with reading ingredient lists and measuring cold water, then progress to supervised preparation and advanced kitchen operations. A written simulation is a learning exercise, not proof of unsupervised physical competency.

Stage focus

Naming ingredients, washing hands, asking for adult help.

Five daily learning activities

  1. Day 1: Read the full lesson and identify safety words.
  2. Day 2: With a trusted adult, make an ingredient and equipment checklist.
  3. Day 3: Practice a safe measuring or planning activity and note what you learned.
  4. Day 4: Plan a family meal, ingredient budget or age-appropriate kitchen task.
  5. Day 5: Demonstrate or safely simulate a skill, complete the reflection and take the assessment.
